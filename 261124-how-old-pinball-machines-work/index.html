How Electromechanical Pinball Works

In February 1977, D. Gottlieb & Co. released Solar City (Model #393), a two-player electromechanical pinball machine with no microprocessors, software, or sound chips. It tracked two players, doubled end-of-ball bonuses, fired four flippers, reset fifteen drop targets, and rang a three-bar aluminum chime box using copper electromagnets, springy metal switches, rotating cams, and 25V alternating current.

Reference: Gottlieb Solar City (Model #393, Feb 1977) Designer: Ed Krynski · Art: Gordon Morison 13 Interactive Figures across 3 Chapters

Under the playfield of a 1970s electromechanical (EM) machine, cloth-braided wiring harnesses link steel-framed relays (electromagnetic switches), solenoids (hollow copper coils that pull a steel rod inward when powered), ratcheting counters, and a rotating cam motor. When the steel pinball bends a springy nickel-silver leaf switch, the contacts touch—closing the circuit so power flows—and trigger coils in sequence. (In electrical switches, closed means contacts touch and power flows; open means separated and off.)

1. Cabinet Zones and Solar City’s Layout

Every EM pinball machine divides its hardware across three zones: the sloped playfield (targets, rollovers, slingshots, pop bumpers, and flippers, with their switches and coils underneath), the lower cabinet (power transformer, relays, score motor, Bonus Unit, tilt pendulum, and chime box), and the upright backbox behind the illuminated glass (mechanical score reels, Player Unit, and 00–90 Match stepper).

Designer Ed Krynski reused the layout from his 1975 El Dorado and 1976 Target Alpha (IPDB #2237): 15 drop targets (a 10-target upper bank and a 5-target right bank), 2 pop bumpers, 2 slingshots (kicking rubber triangles above the lower flippers), and 4 flippers (two 3-inch lower flippers and two 2-inch upper flippers).

Solar City Rules Note · Model #393 (February 1977)

Unlike sibling table El Dorado (1975), where clearing a bank pops the targets back up mid-ball, Solar City’s drop targets stay down until the ball drains. Every drop target scores a flat 300 points (even when adjacent feature lights are lit) and adds 1,000 points to your end-of-ball bonus, which automatically doubles on the last ball.

2. Gravity, Playfield Pitch, and the Spring Plunger

The wooden playfield tilts toward the player so gravity pulls the 11⁄16-inch (27 mm), 2.8-ounce (80 g) steel ball back toward the flippers. Most 1970s EM tables ran at a 4° to 5° pitch to match their slower ball speed, whereas modern computer-controlled solid-state machines typically run at 6.5° to 7°.

To launch the ball up the shooter lane and around the curved upper guide (the top arch), the player pulls back the spring-loaded shooter rod (plunger). Compressing the barrel spring by distance x stores elastic energy E = ½kx², where k is the spring’s stiffness. Because stored energy grows with the square of pull distance, pulling twice as far delivers four times the launch energy—so small pull adjustments separate a soft drop into the top rollover lanes (slots with wire switches the ball rolls over) from a hard shot that rebounds straight out. In Solar City, clearing targets lights the top lane for 5,000 points during play.

3. Dual-Wound Flippers and the End-of-Stroke (EOS) Switch

Gottlieb introduced player-controlled electromechanical flippers in October 1947 on Humpty Dumpty (designed by Harry Mabs), which mounted six short flippers along the playfield sides. By 1977, Gottlieb flippers used a dual-wound solenoid coil—such as the Gottlieb A-5141—to solve a heat problem without electronic transistors.

Batting a 2.8-ounce steel ball back up the playfield takes a brief surge of electrical current (roughly 8 to 10 amps at 25V AC). Players also routinely trap the ball by holding a flipper paddle (called a bat) up for 10 to 15 seconds while aiming. Keeping 10 amps flowing through a copper coil for that long melts the insulating enamel coating on its wire and burns out the coil.

Gottlieb prevented burnout by winding two copper coils onto one spool in series (end to end) and pairing them with a normally closed tungsten End-of-Stroke (EOS) switch beside the flipper crank:

  • Power winding (thick, low-resistance wire, ~1–1.7 Ω): At rest, the closed EOS switch bypasses the thin hold winding. Pressing the cabinet button sends 25V AC straight through the power winding, pulling the solenoid’s steel rod inward in about 25 milliseconds to snap the flipper up.
  • Hold winding (thin, higher-resistance wire, ~6–8 Ω): At the top of the swing, a small hinged lever (a pawl) on the flipper crank pushes the EOS switch open.
  • Low-current hold state: With the bypass open, current must flow through both windings in series, and the steel rod now seated inside the coil raises AC inductive impedance (opposition to alternating current). Current drops from ~10 A to under 1 A—enough magnetic pull to hold the bat upright safely without overheating.
How two cabinet buttons control Solar City’s four flippers

Solar City mounts four flippers (two 3-inch lower flippers and two 2-inch upper flippers) but uses only one button on each side of the cabinet. Behind each button sits a dual-stack flipper switch with two separate contact pairs fed from the same 25V supply lug—one wired to the lower coil and one to the upper coil. When adjusted for staged flipping, a half-press activates the lower flipper first and a full press raises both, letting players trap a ball on the lower bat while swinging the upper bat. Each of the four coils keeps its own under-playfield EOS switch.

4. The Pop Bumper

Active solenoid-driven bumpers carried different brand names in the 1970s: Gottlieb called them pop bumpers, Bally called them “thumper bumpers,” and Williams called them “jet bumpers.” The assembly senses a ball from any 360-degree angle and kicks it away in about 40 milliseconds.

Around the bumper’s base sits a flared plastic skirt whose vertical stem passes through the playfield into a shallow spoon switch underneath. Touching any side of the skirt tilts the stem against the spoon, closing a low-current leaf switch. On 1970s Gottlieb EM tables, that switch pulls in a dedicated pop bumper relay (covered in Part II), which scores 100 points and switches 25V AC to the heavy vertical solenoid below the deck—holding itself on briefly until the bumper completes its stroke so even a glancing hit delivers a full kick.

When the vertical solenoid fires, it yanks a rigid metal-and-fiber yoke (a U-shaped bracket insulated with fiber board) straight downward. That pulls the beveled steel kicker ring down against the upper curve of the ball, wedging it forcefully sideways across the playfield while a steady 6.3V bulb illuminates the translucent bumper cap from above.

5. Drop Target Banks

Ed Krynski popularized large mechanical drop target banks on Gottlieb’s 2001 and Galaxie (1971) and reused the idea across the 1975–1977 El Dorado, Target Alpha, and Solar City series. Solar City mounts 15 molded plastic drop targets: ten across the upper deck and five angled down the right flank.

Each target works as both a scoring trigger and a physical memory bit:

  1. Armed: A molded ledge on the target blade hooks onto a thin stamped-steel retaining shelf on the bank frame, held under tension by a stretched pull-down spring.
  2. Dropped: A pinball impact nudges the blade back roughly 2 mm, slipping it off the retaining shelf so the spring pulls the target flush with the playfield.
  3. Scored and stored: Dropping the blade triggers a brief scoring impulse (registering 300 points and stepping the cabinet’s Bonus Unit forward by 1,000 points) without holding the scoring coil locked on. Meanwhile, its resting down-position leaf switch stays closed to track target-bank progress—so the sunken plastic blade itself remembers the hit without needing a separate relay.
  4. Reset: When the ball drains and the next ball starts, a heavy reset solenoid swings a U-shaped steel reset bar upward under the bank, pushing every dropped target back up until its ledge clicks onto the retaining shelf.

Playfield switches sense ball contact while coils kick the ball back. In Part II, we move down to the cabinet floor to see how the transformer, relays, 1/3-turn score motor, Bonus Unit, and tilt pendulum run the game’s rules without a microprocessor.
