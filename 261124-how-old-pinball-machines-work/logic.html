The Brain Without a Chip

Bolted to a removable plywood board in the bottom of Gottlieb’s 1977 Solar City sits the entire control system: an iron transformer, rows of electromagnetic relays, a rotating cam motor, and ratcheting stepper counters. Together they enforce every game rule, reset cycle, bonus multiplier, and tilt lockout using alternating current and moving switches.

Gottlieb Wiring Schematic #964 25V AC Coils · 6.3V AC Lamps · 1/3-Turn Score Motor

Each cabinet assembly performs one clear job (documented in Gottlieb’s fold-out factory wiring blueprint, Schematic #964, and EM repair guides from PinWiki and PinRepair):

  • Step-down transformer: Lowers 120V wall power into safe, electrically separated lamp and coil power lines (buses).
  • Relays: Electromagnet-driven switches that route current and latch on/off states.
  • Score motor: A timed mechanical sequencer that turns one switch hit into spaced bursts of pulses.
  • Stepper units: Ratcheting rotary counters that track player turns, ball count, and bonus totals.

1. Power Distribution: 120V AC to 25V and 6.3V AC

A laminated-iron step-down transformer bolted to the rear cabinet floor takes standard 120V AC (60 Hz) North American wall power on its primary winding and steps it down into two isolated secondary circuits. Both stay alternating current (AC), because incandescent bulbs and coils work directly on AC without rectifiers (circuits that convert AC to DC). Slow-blow fuses guard each line so a stuck coil blows a fuse instead of cooking the transformer:

  • 6.3V AC lighting bus: Powers the #44 and #47 incandescent bulbs behind Gordon Morison’s backglass, inside pop bumper caps, and beneath translucent plastic playfield windows (called inserts).
  • 25V AC coil and logic bus (typically reading ~24–25V AC under load): Powers flippers, pop bumpers, drop-target resets, relays, score reels, stepper units, chimes, and the score motor.

Flipping the cabinet power switch energizes the transformer, but backglass and playfield lamps stay dark until the H (Hold) Relay—an electromagnetic switch wired to hold itself closed once triggered, covered in Section 2—latches on when you press Start. Its coil circuit passes through normally closed anti-cheat slam switches on the coin door and cabinet floor: kicking the door momentarily bounces a slam switch open, cutting power so the Hold Relay drops out and ends the game.

2. Relays: Leaf-Switch Logic and 1-Bit Memory

A Gottlieb relay pairs a copper electromagnet coil with a hinged steel plate (the armature) that pushes a notched strip of Bakelite (hard, heat-resistant insulating plastic) across a stack of springy nickel-silver leaf switches. When 25V AC energizes the coil, the armature snaps down and flips every switch in its stack at once (labeled A, B, and C on Gottlieb schematics):

  • Normally Open (N.O. / Make “A”): Separated (off) at rest; closes to carry current when energized.
  • Normally Closed (N.C. / Break “B”): Touching (on) at rest; opens to cut current when energized.
  • Make/Break Changeover (“C”): Connects one wire path at rest and flips to an alternate path when energized.

Wiring one of a relay’s own Normally Open switches back to its coil creates a self-holding (lock-in) circuit—a 1-bit mechanical memory cell. A brief pulse energizes the relay, and its own closed switch keeps feeding power to the coil until another switch elsewhere breaks the circuit (used for the H Hold, S Start, and T Tilt relays). For states that stay set for long stretches where constant coil current would overheat the winding—such as Q Game Over, AX Reset Control, or BX Last Ball—Gottlieb used dual-coil latch/trip relays: a latch coil snaps a mechanical hook shut, and a separate trip coil later releases it.

3. The Gottlieb Score Motor: Timed Multi-Pulse Sequencing

A mechanical score reel steps forward only one digit per electrical pulse. When a ball rolls through Solar City’s lit 5,000-point top lane or knocks down a 300-point drop target, the machine must generate five or three evenly spaced pulses rather than one continuous jolt.

That timing comes from the Gottlieb Score Motor: a 25V AC geared electric motor (turning around 25–26 RPM) bolted to the cabinet floor. Its vertical shaft spins stacked Bakelite cam discs—wheels cut with raised bumps (lobes) and notches that press against radial stacks of leaf switches arranged around the rim. On Gottlieb schematics, each switch is addressed by its stack position around the motor (1–4) and vertical level (A–E).

While Bally and Williams EM score motors cycle in 1/2-revolution (180°) turns, every Gottlieb score motor divides one 360° turn into three identical 120° (1/3-revolution) cycles:

  • Index / home switch (Motor 1C): At rest, switch 1C drops into one of three deep notches at 0°, 120°, or 240°, opening (cutting) motor power. When a scoring or start relay nudges the motor out of its notch, 1C closes and powers the motor itself until it falls into the next notch 120° later and stops.
  • Impulse switch stacks (such as 1A): During one 120° turn—roughly 0.8 seconds at 25–26 RPM—five raised lobes on the impulse cam close switch 1A five times for 500- or 5,000-point scores, while a separate three-impulse switch path fires three pulses for 300-point drop targets. Multi-step start resets simply keep running consecutive 1/3-turn cycles until reset relays confirm every score reel has reached zero.

4. Stepper Units: Counting Multi-Step State and Bonuses

Relays store only a single on-or-off bit. To count higher values—which ball is in play, whether Player 1 or Player 2 is shooting, or how many drop targets count toward the end-of-ball bonus—Gottlieb used rotary stepper units.

A stepper unit pairs a stationary Bakelite board lined with brass contact rivets (each wired to a lamp or circuit) with a spring-loaded brass wiper arm mounted on a toothed ratchet wheel. Two opposing solenoids move it:

  • Step-Up coil: Each Solar City drop target hit fires this coil, pushing a hinged metal finger (a pawl) that advances the ratchet wheel by one tooth, moving the wiper to the next rivet and adding +1,000 bonus (up to 15,000 across all 15 targets).
  • Step-Down (subtract) coil: When the ball drains into the outhole (the pocket below the flippers), the score motor steps the Bonus Unit backward one tooth at a time. Each tick triggers the 1,000-point score relay and rings the high chime bar. On the last ball, the Double Bonus relay delivers two 1,000-point pulses per step—awarding 2,000 points per target (up to 30,000 points)—until the wiper reaches rivet 0, where the zero-position switch stack opens the step-down circuit and closes a companion contact signaling the machine to serve the next ball.

5. Motion Detection: Tilt Pendulum and Slam Switch

Lightly nudging the cabinet to steer a ball away from the center drain is part of pinball skill; lifting or shoving the cabinet is penalized. Gottlieb EM machines separate fair nudges from heavy shoves with two opposite switches (alongside a steel ball-roll switch inside the left wall that detects lifting the front of the cabinet):

  • Plumb-bob tilt pendulum (Normally Open): Mounted on the inside left cabinet wall, a weighted conical brass bob hangs through a stationary conductive tilt ring. A light tap sways the bob inside the gap. A hard shove swings it into the ring, closing the circuit to latch the Tilt Relay—cutting power to all four flippers, both pop bumpers, and scoring switches, lighting TILT on the backglass, and forfeiting your accumulated end-of-ball bonus. Operators tune sensitivity with a thumbscrew: sliding the wider base of the cone higher into the ring narrows the clearance.
  • Slam switches (Normally Closed): As seen in Figure 6, weighted anti-cheat switches on the coin door, cabinet floor, underside of the playfield, and backbox bounce open on a violent kick, dropping the Hold Relay and ending the entire active game rather than just one ball.
