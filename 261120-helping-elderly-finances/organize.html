Step 1 of 6

Talk, then get organized

Before you can protect anything, you need two things: your parent's trust and a clear picture of what exists. Here's how to get both without a fight.

  • Time: a few conversations + one afternoon
  • Bring: recent statements, a notebook or laptop

On this page

    Start the conversation (the right way)

    Most parents aren't resisting help. They're resisting losing control. Frame everything around their wishes and your peace of mind, never around their competence.

    1. Pick a calm moment, not a crisis

      Avoid holidays, hospital rooms, and the day after a scam scare. A quiet coffee or a drive works better than a "family meeting."

    2. Lead with yourself

      Mention your own will, your own POA, or a friend's experience. It makes planning normal rather than a judgment about age.

    3. Ask about wishes before numbers

      "Where would you want to live if stairs got hard?" opens more doors than "How much is in your IRA?"

    4. Start with one small, concrete thing

      "Can we find where your papers are?" or "Can we freeze your credit together?" A small win builds trust for the bigger topics.

    5. Include siblings early

      Agree who does what (one handles bills, one handles medical) and how you'll share updates. Surprises breed suspicion.

    6. Write down what you learn and agree

      Send a short summary to everyone afterward. It prevents "that's not what Mom said" later.

    Openers that work

    The "my own planning" opener

    I just updated my will, and it made me realize I don't know where yours is. If something happened, would I know where to look?

    The "scam story" opener

    A friend's mom got a call from someone pretending to be her grandson in jail. She almost sent $8,000. Could we set up a few protections together, so that can't happen to you?

    The "I'm not taking over" opener

    I'm not asking to manage anything. I just want to know who to call and what you'd want if you were ever in the hospital.

    If they push back

    That's fair, and it's your decision. Could we start with just one thing: where you keep your important papers? We can talk about the rest another time.

    Warning signs it's time to step in

    One sign on its own may mean nothing. Two or more, or any sudden change, is a reason to look more closely, gently.

    • Late notices, shut-off warnings, or bills paid twice
    • Unusual withdrawals, especially repeated ATM cash
    • Gift card, wire, crypto, or "Bitcoin ATM" purchases
    • A new "friend," romantic interest, or helper asking about money
    • Piles of unopened mail or sweepstakes and charity letters
    • Confusion about statements they used to handle easily
    • Someone new added to accounts, a deed, or a will
    • Secrecy or defensiveness about a "great opportunity"
    • Bounced checks or an unexpectedly empty account

    If you suspect someone is exploiting them

    Call their bank's fraud department, then contact Adult Protective Services in their county (find it at napsa-now.org) or the National Elder Fraud Hotline: 1-833-372-8311. If they're in immediate danger, call 911.

    Build the family financial map

    The goal is a single document that answers: what exists, where is it, who do we call, and who inherits it? Work through these ten categories together. Record the institution, the last 4 digits only, a phone number, how statements arrive, and any named beneficiary.

    Income

    • Social Security
    • Pensions & annuities
    • Required minimum distributions (RMDs)
    • Rental or part-time income

    Banking

    • Checking & savings
    • CDs and money market
    • Safe deposit box (where's the key?)

    Investments & retirement

    • IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s
    • Brokerage accounts
    • Savings bonds (TreasuryDirect or paper)

    Insurance

    • Medicare + Medigap or Advantage, Part D
    • Life & long-term care
    • Home, auto, umbrella

    Debts

    • Mortgage or reverse mortgage
    • Credit cards
    • Car or personal loans
    • Anything they co-signed

    Property

    • Home deed & property tax
    • Vehicle titles
    • Valuables, collections, firearms

    Recurring bills

    • Utilities, phone, internet
    • Autopays and subscriptions
    • Property tax and insurance premiums

    Their people

    • Attorney, CPA, financial advisor
    • Insurance agent
    • Doctors and pharmacy

    Digital access

    • Phone passcode
    • Primary email account
    • Password manager or password book

    Key documents

    • Will, trust, POA, healthcare proxy
    • Birth & marriage certificates, divorce decrees
    • Military discharge (DD-214)
    • Last 3 years of tax returns

    Record where, not what

    Don't write full account numbers, PINs, or passwords on a paper list that sits on a desk. Write "Password: in the blue notebook in the safe" or store details in a password manager with emergency access. A stolen financial map is a gift to a thief.

    Where to keep the originals

    OptionGood forWatch out for
    Fireproof home safeOriginals of POA, healthcare docs, deeds, titles, certificatesSomeone you trust must know the location and the combination
    Bank safe deposit boxIrreplaceable items, bonds, copiesAccess can be restricted after death or incapacity. Don't keep the only original will or healthcare proxy here. Consider adding you as a co-lessee.
    Attorney's officeOriginal will and trustKeep the firm's name and phone number on the map; firms close and merge
    Password managerLogins, account numbers, scanned documentsSet up emergency access (1Password, Bitwarden and others offer it) so you can get in if needed

    Simplify before you need to

    Every extra account is one more thing to watch, and one more thing to close later. While your parent is fully in charge, help them streamline.

    1. Consolidate accounts

      Two checking accounts and one brokerage are far easier to monitor than seven scattered CDs and three old 401(k)s. Rolling old 401(k)s into one IRA is often a good start (check fees and penalties first).

    2. Trim credit cards to one or two

      Pay off and close store cards. Keep the oldest general card to protect their credit history.

    3. Automate the predictable

      Direct deposit for all income; autopay for utilities, insurance premiums, and minimum card payments. Fewer checks in the mail means fewer checks to steal.

    4. Get duplicate statements or online access

      Many banks and brokerages will mail or email a duplicate statement to an "interested party" with the owner's written permission. It gives you visibility without any control.

    5. Keep paper statements if no one reads email

      Going paperless only helps if someone actually opens those emails. Paper in a known pile is better than unread notifications.
