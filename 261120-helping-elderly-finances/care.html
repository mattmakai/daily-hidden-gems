Step 5 of 6

Plan for long-term care before it's urgent

Long-term care is the largest financial risk most retirees face, and the one most families misunderstand. The biggest surprise: Medicare doesn't pay for it.

  • Time: read now; one planning meeting
  • Who helps: elder law attorney, SHIP counselor, hospital social worker

On this page

    What care costs

    Many people need care for only a short time, but federal estimates suggest about 1 in 5 people turning 65 will need it for more than five years. Plan for the long tail, not the average.

    The "observation status" trap

    Medicare only covers rehab in a nursing facility after three midnights as an admitted inpatient. Time spent "under observation" doesn't count, even if your parent is in a regular hospital bed. Ask every day: "Is my parent admitted as an inpatient, or under observation?" Hospitals must give a written notice (called a MOON) after 24 hours of observation. Medicare Advantage plans may waive the 3-day rule but often require prior authorization.

    How Medicaid pays for care

    Medicaid pays for most long-term nursing home care in the U.S., but only after your parent's resources are nearly exhausted. Rules vary a lot by state; this is the general shape.

    1. Medical need

      A state assessment confirms your parent needs a nursing-home level of care, typically help with several daily activities or significant dementia.

    2. Asset limit

      Countable assets must be very low, often around $2,000 for a single person. The home is usually exempt up to an equity limit if a spouse lives there or your parent intends to return. A car, personal belongings, and a prepaid funeral are generally exempt too.

    3. The 5-year look-back

      Medicaid reviews every financial transfer in the 60 months before applying. Gifts, or sales below fair value, create a penalty period when Medicaid won't pay. That includes "helping out" grandchildren, or putting the house in a child's name.

    4. Spousal protections

      If one parent needs care and the other stays home, the "community spouse" can keep the home, a car, and a protected share of savings and income. Married couples have much more room to plan.

    5. Estate recovery

      After death, the state can seek repayment from the estate, often including the house. Some planning tools can protect it, but only if done correctly and in time.

    If you're paid to provide care, put it in writing

    Money a parent pays a family caregiver can look like a gift under the look-back. A written personal care agreement, at fair market rates and paid as care is provided (not in advance), turns it into legitimate compensation. Keep time logs, and remember it's taxable income to you.

    Don't move assets on your own

    Well-meaning transfers are the most common reason families get hit with Medicaid penalties. Talk to an elder law attorney before gifting, retitling, or "spending down." Done right, planning can protect a meaningful share of savings, especially for married couples.

    Other ways to pay for care

    Existing long-term care insurance

    Find the policy and note the daily benefit, elimination period (days you pay first), benefit period, and any inflation rider.

    If premiums jump, ask about reducing benefits rather than letting it lapse. Notify the insurer as soon as care starts; claims take time.

    Life insurance riders

    Many policies have an accelerated death benefit or chronic-illness rider that pays part of the death benefit early for care. Call the insurer and ask.

    VA Aid & Attendance

    A monthly pension add-on for wartime veterans and surviving spouses who need help with daily activities. Apply through the VA or a free accredited Veterans Service Officer, never a paid "advisor."

    Home equity

    Selling, renting, or borrowing against the home. With a reverse mortgage, the loan generally comes due if the borrower lives away from the home for more than 12 consecutive months, for example in a nursing home.

    PACE programs

    In many areas, the Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly coordinates medical and home care so people eligible for nursing-home care can stay home. Search "PACE" on Medicare.gov.

    Free local help

    Your parent's Area Agency on Aging (Eldercare Locator, 1-800-677-1116) knows local programs, waivers, and waitlists. SHIP counselors give free, unbiased Medicare advice.

    Choosing a facility

    1. Check ratings and inspection reports

      Use Medicare Care Compare for nursing homes (staffing, inspections, star ratings). For assisted living, check your state's licensing agency.

    2. Visit more than once, and unannounced

      Go at mealtime and in the evening or on a weekend. Notice smells, call-light response times, and whether residents are engaged or parked in hallways.

    3. Ask the hard questions

      Staff-to-resident ratio on nights and weekends? Staff turnover? What happens when money runs out: do they accept Medicaid, and will your parent have to move rooms?

    4. Talk to the Long-Term Care Ombudsman

      Every state has a free ombudsman program that advocates for residents and knows each facility's complaint history.

    5. Read the admission agreement carefully

      See the warning below.

    Never sign as "responsible party" or guarantor

    Federal law prohibits Medicare- and Medicaid-certified nursing homes from requiring a third party to guarantee payment as a condition of admission. Yet admission papers often contain a "responsible party" line that can make you personally liable. If you sign, write "as agent under power of attorney" after your name, and strike any personal-guarantee or mandatory-arbitration language you're not comfortable with.
