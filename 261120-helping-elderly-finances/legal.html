Step 4 of 6

Sign the papers while they're healthy

A few documents decide whether you can pay a bill, talk to a doctor, or settle an estate. Once a parent loses capacity, they can't sign them, and the alternative is court-supervised guardianship: slow, expensive, and public.

  • Time: 2–6 weeks with an attorney
  • Who helps: an elder law or estate planning attorney

On this page

    Who can act, and when

    Each document works in a different window of time. This is the single most misunderstood part of planning: a power of attorney ends at death, and a will does nothing until then.

    The gap people fall into

    Between the moment of death and the court appointing an executor, nobody has authority over accounts in your parent's sole name. The POA has ended, and the will isn't active yet. Accounts with beneficiaries (POD/TOD) and trust assets avoid this gap entirely.

    The five core documents

    1. Durable financial power of attorney

    Names an agent to manage money and property. "Durable" means it keeps working if your parent becomes incapacitated, which is the entire point.

    • Prefer "effective immediately" over "springing." A springing POA only activates after doctors certify incapacity, which can delay access for weeks in a crisis.
    • Make sure it covers: banking, real estate, retirement accounts, taxes, digital assets, government benefits, and funding a trust.
    • Include gifting authority if Medicaid planning might ever be needed.
    • Name a backup agent.

    2. Healthcare proxy + living will

    The healthcare proxy (or healthcare POA) names someone to make medical decisions. The living will records wishes about life support, feeding tubes, and resuscitation. Many states combine them in one "advance directive."

    • Add a HIPAA authorization so doctors and insurers can share information with you even before your parent is incapacitated.
    • For a serious illness, ask the doctor about a POLST/MOLST: a signed medical order emergency crews will follow.

    3. Will

    Names an executor and says who gets assets that don't already have a beneficiary or co-owner. Without one, state law decides.

    • Know where the original is. Most courts require the signed original, not a copy.
    • Review after a death, divorce, move to a new state, or major change in assets.

    4. Revocable living trust (optional, often worth it)

    Holds assets during life; a successor trustee steps in at incapacity or death with no court involvement. Avoids probate, keeps things private, and especially helps with real estate in multiple states.

    • The trust must be funded. Accounts and the house must be retitled into it. An unfunded trust is a common, costly mistake.

    5. Beneficiary designations

    These override the will. Retirement accounts, life insurance, and annuities pass by beneficiary form, as do bank and brokerage accounts set up as payable-on-death (POD) or transfer-on-death (TOD).

    • Check every account for ex-spouses, deceased beneficiaries, or minors.
    • Name contingent (backup) beneficiaries.
    • Many states allow a TOD deed for the house and TOD titles for vehicles.

    Get the POA accepted now, not during the crisis

    Banks and brokerages sometimes balk at an older or unfamiliar POA. Once it's signed, take a copy to each institution while your parent can come along. Ask them to review it and keep it on file, and fill out any form of their own they require. Many states now require institutions to accept a valid statutory POA, but it's far easier to sort out early.

    Where a POA doesn't work

    Several agencies run their own authorization systems and will not accept a power of attorney. Set these up separately.

    The joint-account shortcut that backfires

    Adding a child as a joint owner feels like the easy fix. It often creates bigger problems. Compare your options:

    Usually what you want Usually a drawback

    Don't put the house in your name "to keep it simple"

    Gifting the home to a child during life can lose the step-up in tax basis (a potentially huge capital gains bill when it's sold), trigger Medicaid's 5-year look-back, and expose the house to your creditors. A TOD deed or a trust usually does the job better. Ask an elder law attorney first.

    Plan their digital legacy

    Apple

    Settings → [their name] → Sign-In & Security → Legacy Contact. You'll receive an access key to request their data after death.

    Google

    Set up Inactive Account Manager at myaccount.google.com/inactive to share data with you after a period of inactivity.

    Facebook

    Settings → Memorialization → choose a legacy contact, or ask for the account to be deleted after death.

    Password manager & phone

    Turn on the manager's emergency-access feature. Write the phone passcode in a sealed envelope with the will.

    How to get it done

    1. Find an elder law or estate planning attorney

      Search the National Academy of Elder Law Attorneys directory or your state bar's referral service. Many offer flat-fee planning packages. Low-income seniors can find free legal aid through the Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116).

    2. Bring the financial map

      The attorney needs to know what's owned, how it's titled, and who the current beneficiaries are. Build the map first.

    3. Let your parent meet the attorney alone

      The attorney represents your parent, not you. Private time protects the documents against later claims of undue influence by a sibling or anyone else.

    4. Sign with the required witnesses and notary

      Rules vary by state. A document signed incorrectly may be rejected exactly when you need it.

    5. Distribute copies

      Healthcare documents go to their doctors, the hospital, and the healthcare agent. Bring the POA to each bank. Keep originals in a known, safe place.

    6. Review every 3–5 years

      And after any death, divorce, diagnosis, or move to another state.

    On a tight budget?

    Every state has a free statutory form for advance directives; AARP and CaringInfo publish them by state. Many states also have a statutory POA form. They're better than nothing, but an attorney is worth it for POAs, trusts, and anything involving Medicaid.
