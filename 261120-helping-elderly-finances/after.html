After a death: one step at a time

You don't have to do this all at once. Very little is truly urgent in the first days. This checklist sorts the tasks by when they actually need to happen, so you can grieve first.

  • Keep handy: the financial map, the will, certified death certificates

    What goes through probate (and what doesn't)

    Probate is the court process of validating a will and transferring assets. Many assets skip it entirely, which is why beneficiary designations and trusts matter so much.

    Passes directly, no probate

    • Accounts with a payable-on-death (POD) or transfer-on-death (TOD) beneficiary
    • IRAs, 401(k)s, annuities, and life insurance with a living beneficiary
    • Joint accounts and property with right of survivorship
    • Anything titled in a living trust
    • Real estate with a TOD deed (in states that allow it)

    Goes through probate

    • Accounts in their name alone with no beneficiary
    • Real estate in their name alone
    • Vehicles (in most states, though many offer small-estate shortcuts)
    • Personal belongings, collections, jewelry
    • Accounts whose named beneficiary already died

    What happens to their debts

    Generally, you don't inherit debt

    Debts are paid from the estate before heirs receive anything. If the estate runs out, most remaining debts go unpaid. Family members usually aren't personally responsible.

    The exceptions

    You may owe if you co-signed, were a joint account holder (not just an authorized user), live in a community property state as the surviving spouse, or signed a facility agreement as a personal guarantor.

    Know your rights with debt collectors

    Collectors may contact the executor, but they can't pressure you to pay from your own money or imply you're required to. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (consumerfinance.gov) explains your rights. Note that Medicaid may also make a claim against the estate for care it paid for.
