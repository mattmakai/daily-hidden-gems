Step 2 of 6

Lock the doors scammers use most

Identity thieves and scammers rely on a handful of openings: unfrozen credit, unclaimed government accounts, a hijackable phone number, and a moment of panic. Close each one.

  • Time: about 2 hours, best done side by side
  • You'll need: their SSN, date of birth, addresses for the past 2 years, phone & email

On this page

    1. Freeze their credit at all three bureaus

    A credit freeze stops anyone (including a thief with their Social Security number) from opening a new credit card, loan, or account in their name. It's free, it doesn't affect their credit score, and their existing cards keep working. If they ever need new credit, they can lift the freeze online in minutes.

    1. Create an account at each bureau in your parent's name

      Use their email address (or a shared family one you both can access). They'll answer identity questions about past addresses and loans, so sit with them.

    2. Place the freeze

      Online freezes take effect within an hour. By phone it's about as fast; by mail, a few business days.

    3. Save the login or PIN for each bureau

      Store it in the password manager or with the financial map. They'll need it to temporarily "thaw" the freeze for a new card, a car loan, or some utility and phone sign-ups.

    4. Optional: freeze the specialty bureaus too

      These cover account types the big three don't:

      • ChexSystems new bank accounts · 1-800-428-9623
      • NCTUE phone & utility accounts · 1-866-343-2821
      • Innovis 4th credit bureau · 1-800-540-2505

    Acting for a parent who can't do it themselves

    If your parent can't participate, you can place a freeze on their behalf as their agent under a power of attorney or as guardian. Each bureau requires mailed proof: a copy of the POA or court order plus ID for both of you. That's one more reason to get a POA signed early.

    2. Claim their government accounts first

    A thief with a stolen Social Security number can create an online account in your parent's name and redirect their benefits or tax refund. Once your parent has created the account, that door is closed.

    my Social Security

    Create at ssa.gov/myaccount (via Login.gov or ID.me). Check that the direct deposit and address are correct.

    Any change to direct deposit they didn't make is a major red flag.

    IRS Identity Protection PIN

    Get one at irs.gov/ippin. It's a 6-digit code required to file a return under their SSN, which blocks fake returns. A new PIN is issued each year.

    Medicare.gov account

    Lets you review claims and switch to electronic Medicare Summary Notices. Look for services, equipment, or tests they never received.

    USPS Informed Delivery

    Sign up at informeddelivery.usps.com. A daily email shows scans of arriving mail. It also prevents a thief from signing up first to watch their mail.

    3. Lock down their phone and email

    Their phone number and email are the master keys: password resets and bank codes go there. Protect them like you'd protect a bank account.

    Mobile number lock / port-out PINCall the carrier or use its app. It's called Number Lock (Verizon), SIM Protection (T-Mobile), or Wireless Account Lock (AT&T). Stops "SIM swap" thieves from moving the number to their phone.Also set an account PIN required for any changes.
    2-step verification on emailTurn it on for their main email (Gmail, Outlook, Yahoo, iCloud). Set the recovery phone to theirs and the recovery email to yours.
    Password managerOne strong master password; unique passwords everywhere else. Turn on its emergency-access feature with you as the contact.
    Silence unknown callersiPhone: Settings → Apps → Phone → Silence Unknown Callers (older iOS: Settings → Phone). Android: Phone app → Settings → Caller ID & spam → Filter spam calls. Real callers can leave a voicemail.

    4. The scam playbook

    Almost every scam aimed at older adults uses the same three levers: urgency ("act now"), secrecy ("don't tell anyone"), and an unusual way to pay (gift cards, wire, crypto, gold, or cash handed to a courier). Teach the pattern, not just the individual scams.

    Most common

    Government impersonation

    "Your Social Security number is suspended." "You owe the IRS." "Your Medicare card expires."

    Agencies contact you by mail first and never demand payment by phone.

    Fastest-growing

    Bank or "fraud department" impersonation

    "Your account is compromised. Move your money to a safe account." Often follows a fake text alert.

    No real bank asks you to move money to protect it.

    Uses AI voice cloning

    Grandparent / family emergency

    A panicked "grandchild" in jail or a hospital needs bail money now, and begs them not to tell Mom.

    The family safe word defeats this.

    Tech support

    A pop-up or call says their computer is infected. "Call Microsoft at this number." Then remote access and a "refund" scam.

    Romance & friendship

    A warm online relationship that never meets in person and eventually needs money for an emergency or an investment.

    Investment & crypto

    A new "friend" shows them a trading app with huge gains. Withdrawals require "taxes" or "fees." It's all fake.

    The four-step response, for every suspicious call, text, or email

    1. StopDon't click, don't pay, don't confirm any information.
    2. Hang upReal agencies and banks are fine with it. Scammers aren't.
    3. Call backOn a number you already trust: the back of the card, a statement, or the official website.
    4. Tell someoneCall you (or another trusted person) before any money moves.

    Before you send money to anyone

    No real government agency, bank, or tech company will ever:

    • Ask you to pay with gift cards, wire transfer, cryptocurrency, gold, or a Bitcoin ATM
    • Send a courier to pick up cash or valuables
    • Tell you to move money to a "safe account"
    • Ask you to keep it secret from your family or bank
    • Threaten arrest, deportation, or losing benefits over the phone
    Hang up and call:
    Phone:

    Remember the family safe word. If a caller can't say it, it isn't us, no matter how real the voice sounds.

    The family safe word

    Pick a word or phrase that isn't on social media (not a pet's name). Everyone in the family knows it. Any urgent call asking for money, even in a voice that sounds exactly like a grandchild, must include it. Practice it once, out loud, so it feels normal.

    5. If it already happened: act in this order

    1. Call the bank or card company's fraud line

      Use the number on the card. Ask them to stop or recall any pending payment, freeze the account, and issue new cards. Speed matters most for wires and Zelle.

    2. Change passwords and remove remote access

      If a "tech support" caller was on the computer, have it checked and uninstall any remote-access app they installed. Change email and bank passwords from a different, clean device.

    3. Report identity theft at IdentityTheft.gov

      The FTC's site creates an official report and a personalized recovery plan with pre-filled letters.

    4. Report the fraud

      ReportFraud.ftc.gov for scams, IC3.gov for anything online, and the National Elder Fraud Hotline, 1-833-372-8311, for help deciding what to do next.

    5. File a local police report

      Some banks and insurers require one before reimbursing losses.

    6. Beware the "recovery" scam

      Scammers sell lists of past victims. Anyone who promises to recover lost money for an upfront fee is running a second scam.

    Lead with kindness

    Victims often hide losses out of shame, which lets scams continue. Make it clear that these are professional criminals who fool people of every age, and that you're glad they told you.
