Step 3 of 6

See problems in hours, not months

Most financial exploitation is discovered weeks or months late, when a statement finally gets read. The right alerts, sent to your parent and to you, shrink that window to minutes.

  • Time: about 1 hour
  • You'll need: their online banking logins and phone

On this page

    What good alerts look like

    9:41
    Bank nowATM withdrawal of $800.00 at 5th & Main. Not you? Reply NO.
    Bank 2m agoNew Zelle recipient "J. Miller" added to your account.
    Credit card 14m ago$500.00 purchase at GIFT CARD MALL.
    Email security 1h agoYour password was changed on a new Windows device.

    Each of these alerts on its own could be innocent. Together, within an hour, they're the fingerprint of a scam in progress, and they arrive while there's still time to call the bank.

    The goal isn't to watch every coffee purchase. It's to be notified about the few events that scammers can't avoid:

    • Money leaving in large or unusual ways (ATM cash, wires, Zelle, gift cards)
    • New people being added (payees, recipients, authorized users)
    • The account itself changing (password, phone, email, address, linked bank)

    Choose the lightest level of visibility that works

    Start at the lowest rung that gives you enough warning. Move up only when needed, and always with your parent's agreement.

    1. Level 1Alerts to them

      Alerts go only to your parent's phone or email. Good for sharp, independent parents.

      Everyone should have this
    2. Level 2Alerts copied to you

      The same alerts also reach you, by adding your contact as a second recipient or by email forwarding.

      The sweet spot for most families
    3. Level 3View-only access

      You can see balances and transactions but can't move money. Through duplicate statements, a bank's view-only feature, or a monitoring service.

      When bills start slipping
    4. Level 4Acting as agent

      You pay bills and manage accounts under a power of attorney. Keep records and keep their money separate from yours.

      When they can't manage safely

    Alert recipes, account by account

    Log in to each account with your parent, find Alerts or Notifications (usually under Profile or Settings), and turn these on. Set dollar thresholds around twice their typical transaction, so alerts stay meaningful.

    Bank (checking & savings)

    AlertSettingWhy it matters
    Withdrawal or debit over $X$250–$500Catches large unauthorized payments
    Any ATM withdrawalAllSudden cash use is a classic sign of a scam or exploitation
    Wire transfer or Zelle sentAllThese payments are nearly impossible to reverse
    New payee or Zelle recipient addedAllScammers add themselves before moving money
    Password, phone, email, or address changedAllThe first sign of an account takeover
    Login from a new deviceAllSomeone else may have their password
    Large depositOver $1,000Fake "overpayment" and loan scams start with a deposit
    Low balanceBelow one month of billsPrevents bounced payments and overdraft fees
    Daily balance summaryDaily emailA quick pulse check you can scan in seconds

    Also ask the bank: Can a second phone or email receive alerts? Can daily ATM and Zelle limits be lowered? Do they offer a trusted-contact designation? Many banks now do.

    How to get copies of their alerts

    Option A · simplest

    Add you as a second recipient

    Many banks and card issuers allow more than one email or phone for alerts. Look for "add another email" in alert settings, or ask the branch.

    Option B · works anywhere

    Forward alert emails automatically

    In Gmail: Settings → See all settings → Forwarding and POP/IMAP → Add a forwarding address (you confirm by email). Then search for the bank's alert emails, choose Create filter → Forward it to your address.

    Outlook and Apple Mail have similar "rules."

    Option C · paper trail

    Duplicate statements

    Ask each institution to mail or email a duplicate monthly statement to you as an "interested party." Requires your parent's signature, gives you no control.

    Option D · hands-off

    A monitoring service

    Paid services such as EverSafe or Carefull connect read-only to accounts, learn normal patterns, and alert a trusted family member to anomalies. Compare features and privacy policies before signing up.

    Don't share their passwords to "just log in as them"

    Logging in with your parent's credentials usually violates the bank's terms and can make it harder to get fraud losses reimbursed. Use alerts, duplicate statements, a bank's own view-only access, or a power of attorney instead.

    A simple review routine

    Alerts only work if someone looks. Put these on your calendar.

    Weekly · 5 min

    • Skim forwarded alerts
    • Glance at the mail preview
    • Ask: "Any strange calls this week?"

    Monthly · 15 min

    • Review statements for new payees
    • Check balance trend
    • Confirm bills were paid
    • Note anything odd in a shared log

    Quarterly · 30 min

    • Pull all three credit reports
    • Review Medicare Summary Notice
    • Check Social Security deposit & address

    Yearly · 1 hr

    • New IRS IP PIN (January)
    • Review beneficiaries
    • Update the financial map
    • Revisit alert thresholds
