Score, Sound & 1977 Legacy

Electromechanical pinball machines displayed scores on spinning numbered drums behind illuminated glass, made sound by striking aluminum bars over a wooden resonance box, and signaled free games with a solenoid knocker against the cabinet wall. This final chapter covers those output mechanisms and the documented history of Gottlieb’s 1977 Solar City.

Score Reels · 3-Bar Chime Box · 00–90 Match Gottlieb Model #393 · February 1977

1. Mechanical Score Reels: Counting, Carrying, and Zeroing

Behind Gordon Morison’s backglass on Solar City sit two horizontal rows of white numbered plastic drums—one bank for Player 1 and one for Player 2. Because Solar City scores in multiples of ten, the rightmost “0” is printed permanently on the backglass while four mechanical score reels per player display the 10s, 100s, 1,000s, and 10,000s digits (up to 99,990, plus a 100,000 wrap-around lamp).

Each drum is stepped by a 25V AC solenoid pushing a hinged metal finger (a ratchet pawl), rotating the drum by exactly 36 degrees (one-tenth of a turn) per pulse to advance from 0 through 9. A three-switch stack on the side of every drum handles carry-over and game-start zeroing:

  • 9th-position carry switch: Stays open on digits 0–8 and closes only at 9. On the next pulse, that closed switch energizes the next higher score relay (so the 10s reel at 9 trips the 100-point relay), cascading 09,990 + 10 cleanly across all four drums to 10,000.
  • Zero-position runout switch: Stays closed on digits 1–9 and drops open into a cam notch at 0. At game start, Gottlieb’s Z1 reset relay pulses all four reels in each player’s bank together through the score motor (four-player Gottliebs add a second Z2 relay for Players 3 and 4). Each drum steps forward until it reaches 0, opens its runout switch, and stops.
  • Zero-position end-of-reset switch: Wired across every drum in series and closes only at 0. Once every single reel reaches zero, this completed chain drops the Z1 reset relay out so the game can serve Ball 1.

2. The Chime Box, 00–90 Match Unit, and Replay Conversion

Before electronic sound boards arrived in 1978, Gottlieb machines produced sound using a three-bar chime unit (Gottlieb assembly B-12023) bolted to the inner right cabinet wall. Three cut aluminum tone bars (A-12016/17/18) rest on soft rubber-or-foam rings (grommets) above a multi-chambered wooden resonance box sized for each note. Underneath each bar sits a vertical 25V AC solenoid wired alongside the matching score relay:

  • 10-point bar (low tone): Rings on rollover switches, rubber rebounds, and slingshots.
  • 100-point bar (mid tone): Rings on pop bumpers and pulses three times on every 300-point drop target hit.
  • 1,000-point bar (high tone): Rings on every tick of the end-of-ball bonus countdown and five times on the lit 5,000-point top lane.

At Game Over, the backbox reveals the 00–90 Match Unit—a hidden 10-position rotary stepper that advances during gameplay (driven by the 10-point scoring relay) rather than spinning at the end. When the final ball drains, the backglass lights the Match digit (00 through 90) where that wiper happened to stop. In Replay mode, if it matches the last two digits of your final score, the replay knocker fires a steel plunger into a cabinet bracket with a sharp crack and adds one free game to the credit wheel.

Gottlieb’s 1977 Solar City sales flyer promoted operator conversion between Replay and Add-a-Ball operation. Where local laws banned arcades from awarding free games, operators could move a rectangular multi-pin jumper plug (a Jones plug) in the backbox so a lit playfield Special (the table’s top award) granted an Extra Ball during play instead of a free credit—while switching off the end-of-game Replay Match.

3. Case Study: Gottlieb’s Solar City (February 1977)

Released in February 1977 by D. Gottlieb & Co. from its Northlake, Illinois plant, Solar City (Model #393, Schematic #964, Internet Pinball Database #2237) dressed Ed Krynski’s 15-target playfield in retro science-fantasy artwork by Gordon Morison—depicting futuristic domed towers and figures beneath radiant suns. It shipped right as microprocessor-based machines with calculator-style 7-segment digital displays began replacing relay tables.

Factory production archives cited on IPDB and Pinside (a major pinball collector database and forum) record 2,525 production machines, while the Internet Pinball Serial Number Database (IPSND) lists 2,634. Restoration notes attribute the 109-unit difference to 109 pre-production sample machines built in 1976 alongside the 2,525-unit main run.

4. The Creators: Ed Krynski and Gordon Morison

Solar City paired Gottlieb’s lead mechanical designer with its principal 1970s illustrator—both later inducted into the Pinball Expo Hall of Fame and preserved in collections such as the Pacific Pinball Museum.

Game Designer · 1927–2004

Ed Krynski

Born September 12, 1927 in Illinois, Krynski served in the U.S. Navy during World War II and designed amusement games for J.H. Keeney & Co. before joining Gottlieb around 1963. Succeeding Wayne Neyens as lead designer, he created more than 200 pinball machines, including Royal Flush (1976), Sinbad (1978), Genie (1979), The Amazing Spider-Man (1980), and his final table, El Dorado: City of Gold (1984).

Krynski emphasized aimed shots and drop-target sequences over random ball bounces. He retired from Gottlieb in 1984 and was inducted into the Pinball Expo Hall of Fame on October 18, 2003.

Principal Artist · 1930–2000

Gordon Alexander Morison

Born April 27, 1930 in Indiana, Morison served in the Korean War and trained at Chicago’s Advertising Posters Company. Following longtime Gottlieb artist Roy Parker (1899–1966) and his immediate successor Art Stenholm, Morison served as Gottlieb’s principal artist from 1971 (Galaxie) through 1980 (Asteroid Annie and the Aliens), illustrating roughly 137 machines—including Cleopatra (1977), Genie (1979), and Totem (1979)—signed with his initials “GAM.”

After leaving pinball, he illustrated comics including Slimer! and the Real Ghostbusters for NOW Comics. He was inducted into the Pinball Expo Hall of Fame on October 24, 1998.

5. The Seven-Machine El Dorado Family Tree

Solar City’s 15-target, 4-flipper playfield debuted in the single-player Western table El Dorado (1975). Gottlieb reused that playfield across seven titles over nine years—covering single-player, two-player, four-player, Add-a-Ball, export, and promotional editions—before reviving it in 1984 as the microprocessor-based (Gottlieb System 80A) table El Dorado: City of Gold.

6. Gottlieb’s 1977 Turning Point

By the time Solar City shipped in February 1977, D. Gottlieb & Co. was closing fifty years of family ownership. Columbia Pictures had agreed to acquire the company in December 1976 (completing the purchase in early 1977), kicking off an industry-wide shift from relay-wired electromechanical games toward microprocessor circuit boards and licensed pop-culture themes.

  1. 1927
    D. Gottlieb & Co. Founded

    David Gottlieb launches his amusement machine company in Chicago, Illinois.

  2. 1931
    Baffle Ball

    Coin-operated countertop pinball spreads rapidly across the United States during the Great Depression.

  3. 1947
    Humpty Dumpty — First Player Flippers

    Harry Mabs’s player-controlled electromechanical flippers shift pinball from chance to skill.

  4. 1963–72
    EM Expansion & Northlake Plant

    Ed Krynski joins around 1963, Gordon Morison becomes principal artist in 1971, and Gottlieb relocates production from Chicago to Northlake, Illinois in 1972.

  5. 1976–77
    Columbia Pictures Acquisition & Solar City

    Columbia Pictures agrees to acquire Gottlieb in December 1976 (closing in early 1977). Solar City ships in February 1977 near the end of Gottlieb’s pure EM production.

  6. 1982–83
    Coca-Cola / Mylstar Electronics

    Coca-Cola acquires Columbia Pictures (1982) and renames the coin-op division Mylstar Electronics (1983).

  7. 1984
    Mylstar Shutdown, Premier Technology & Krynski’s Departure

    El Dorado: City of Gold launches across the handover (early samples under Mylstar in September; main run under Premier Technology after its October purchase of Mylstar’s pinball assets), and Ed Krynski retires.

  8. 1996
    End of Manufacturing

    Premier Technology closes operations, ending nearly seventy years of Gottlieb-branded pinball manufacturing.

No complete census exists for surviving Solar City cabinets; the 54 registered serial numbers reflect voluntary owner submissions rather than the total surviving count. Condition drives pricing heavily after nearly five decades: flaking on Morison’s silkscreened (ink-printed) backglass, playfield wear, chipped exterior paint (originally applied with factory cabinet stencils), and worn relay contacts separate project machines from restored examples selling well above baseline estimates. Owners can log and compare stamped cabinet serial numbers via IPSND and Pinside.
